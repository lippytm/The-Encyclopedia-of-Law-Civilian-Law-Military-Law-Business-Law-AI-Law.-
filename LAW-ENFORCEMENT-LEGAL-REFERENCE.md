## Introduction

Law enforcement law governs the authority, conduct, limitations, and accountability of police officers, sheriffs, federal agents, and all persons empowered by the state to enforce laws, preserve public order, and protect citizens. In the United States, law enforcement operates within a layered constitutional and statutory framework at the federal, state, and local levels.

This reference covers the foundational legal principles, landmark cases, and applicable statutes that define and constrain law enforcement activity in the United States.

---

## Constitutional Framework

### Fourth Amendment — Search & Seizure
The Fourth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution protects individuals from unreasonable searches and seizures. It requires that warrants be supported by probable cause, be issued by a neutral magistrate, and particularly describe the place to be searched and persons or things to be seized.

**Key requirements:**
- Probable cause must exist for a warrant
- Warrantless searches are presumptively unreasonable (subject to exceptions)
- The Exclusionary Rule bars evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment (*Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643 (1961))

### Fifth Amendment — Self-Incrimination & Due Process
- No person shall be compelled to be a witness against themselves in a criminal case
- Provides due process protections against arbitrary government action
- Grand jury indictment required for serious federal crimes
- Basis for *Miranda v. Arizona* warnings (384 U.S. 436 (1966))

### Sixth Amendment — Right to Counsel
- Right to a speedy and public trial by an impartial jury
- Right to be informed of the nature and cause of accusations
- Right to confront witnesses
- Right to assistance of counsel (*Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963))

### Eighth Amendment — Cruel and Unusual Punishment
Prohibits excessive bail, excessive fines, and cruel and unusual punishment. Applied to conditions of confinement and use-of-force standards.

### Fourteenth Amendment — Equal Protection & Due Process
- Applies Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, and Eighth Amendment protections to the states
- Prohibits discriminatory law enforcement practices
- Substantive due process protects against arbitrary deprivations of liberty

## State & Local Law Enforcement Authority

### Police Power
State and local law enforcement derive authority from the states' inherent "police power" — the power to enact and enforce laws to protect the health, safety, morals, and general welfare of the public. This power is not granted by the federal government but is reserved to the states under the Tenth Amendment.

### Sheriffs
Sheriffs are typically county-level elected law enforcement officials. In many states, the Sheriff is the chief law enforcement officer of the county and has authority independent of the municipal police.

### Municipal Police Departments
City and town police departments are creatures of local government, operating under authority delegated by state law. Officers must comply with:
- State penal codes and criminal procedure statutes
- Local ordinances
- Department policies and collective bargaining agreements
- Constitutional requirements

### State Police & Highway Patrol
State police/highway patrol agencies typically have statewide jurisdiction and often handle:
- Traffic enforcement on state highways
- Rural area policing
- Major crimes investigation
- Assistance to local agencies

## Use of Force

### Legal Standard
The legal standard for police use of force is found in the Fourth Amendment reasonableness test established in *Graham v. Connor*, 490 U.S. 386 (1989). Courts evaluate reasonableness from the perspective of a reasonable officer on the scene, not with 20/20 hindsight.

**Factors considered under *Graham*:**
1. The severity of the crime at issue
2. Whether the suspect poses an immediate threat to officers or others
3. Whether the suspect is actively resisting arrest or attempting to evade

### Use of Deadly Force
*Tennessee v. Garner*, 471 U.S. 1 (1985) — Officers may not use deadly force to prevent the escape of a fleeing felony suspect unless:
- The officer has probable cause to believe the suspect poses a significant threat of death or serious physical injury to the officer or others

### Use-of-Force Continuum
Many agencies use a continuum or matrix to guide officers in selecting appropriate force levels:
1. Officer presence
2. Verbal commands
3. Soft control techniques
4. Hard control techniques / OC spray / Taser
5. Deadly force

### Qualified Immunity
Officers sued in their individual capacities for civil rights violations may assert qualified immunity as a defense. Immunity applies unless the officer violated a "clearly established" statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known (*Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800 (1982)).

## Search & Seizure

### Warrant Requirement
As a general rule, searches and seizures without a warrant are per se unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment.

**Warrant requirements:**
- Probable cause
- Neutral and detached magistrate
- Particularity (place to be searched, items to be seized)

### Exceptions to the Warrant Requirement
| Exception | Description | Leading Case |
|-----------|-------------|--------------|
| Search incident to lawful arrest | Search of person and area within immediate control | *Chimel v. California*, 395 U.S. 752 (1969) |
| Plain view | Officer lawfully present, incriminating nature immediately apparent | *Horton v. California*, 496 U.S. 128 (1990) |
| Exigent circumstances | Emergency: imminent destruction of evidence, hot pursuit, threat to safety | *Mincey v. Arizona*, 437 U.S. 385 (1978) |
| Consent | Voluntary, knowing consent to search | *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973) |
| Terry stops (stop and frisk) | Reasonable articulable suspicion; pat-down for weapons | *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968) |
| Automobile exception | Probable cause vehicle contains contraband or evidence | *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925) |
| Inventory search | Standardized procedure search of impounded vehicle | *South Dakota v. Opperman*, 428 U.S. 364 (1976) |
| Border search | No warrant or suspicion required at border | *United States v. Ramsey*, 431 U.S. 606 (1977) |

### Electronic Surveillance & Digital Searches
- **Electronic Communications Privacy Act (ECPA), 18 U.S.C. §§ 2510–2523** — Governs wiretapping and electronic surveillance
- **Stored Communications Act (SCA), 18 U.S.C. §§ 2701–2713** — Governs access to stored electronic communications
- **Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA), 50 U.S.C. §§ 1801–1885** — Governs foreign intelligence surveillance
- ***Carpenter v. United States*, 138 S. Ct. 2206 (2018)** — Warrant required for cell-site location information (CSLI)
- ***Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373 (2014)** — Warrant required to search cell phone incident to arrest

## Arrest Law

### Probable Cause
An arrest requires probable cause — a reasonable belief, based on articulable facts, that a person has committed or is committing a crime. Probable cause is more than a hunch but less than the preponderance standard.

### Arrest Warrants
An arrest warrant is issued by a neutral magistrate upon a sworn showing of probable cause. A warrant is required to arrest a person in their own home absent consent or exigent circumstances (*Payton v. New York*, 445 U.S. 573 (1980)).

### Warrantless Arrests
Officers may make a warrantless arrest in public places when:
- A felony has been committed and the officer has probable cause to believe the suspect committed it
- A misdemeanor is committed in the officer's presence

### Citizen's Arrest
Most states retain the common-law right of a private citizen to arrest another person who has committed a felony in their presence, or when a felony has been committed and the citizen has reasonable grounds to believe the person is the perpetrator. State statutes vary significantly.

### Post-Arrest Requirements
- Booking and processing
- Prompt presentment before a magistrate (Federal: Rule 5, Fed. R. Crim. P.; state equivalents)
- Bail determination
- Right to counsel attaches at critical stages of prosecution (*Massiah v. United States*, 377 U.S. 201 (1964))

## Interrogation & Confessions

### Miranda Rights
*Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436 (1966) — Before custodial interrogation, police must advise suspects:
1. You have the right to remain silent
2. Anything you say can and will be used against you in a court of law
3. You have the right to an attorney
4. If you cannot afford an attorney, one will be appointed for you

**Custody** is determined by whether a reasonable person would feel free to leave the encounter. **Interrogation** includes express questioning and its functional equivalent.

**Invoking Miranda:**
- Invocation of the right to silence must be scrupulously honored (*Michigan v. Mosley*, 423 U.S. 96 (1975))
- Invocation of right to counsel requires cessation of questioning until counsel present (*Edwards v. Arizona*, 451 U.S. 477 (1981))

### Voluntariness
A confession must be voluntary — the product of a rational intellect and free will — to be admissible. Coerced confessions violate the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments.

### Public Safety Exception
Officers may ask questions necessary to secure their safety or the safety of the public before Miranda warnings in an emergency (*New York v. Quarles*, 467 U.S. 649 (1984)).

## Civil Rights & Law Enforcement

### 42 U.S.C. § 1983 — Civil Action for Deprivation of Rights
The primary vehicle for civil rights lawsuits against state and local law enforcement officers. Plaintiffs must prove:
1. The defendant acted under color of state law
2. The conduct deprived the plaintiff of rights secured by the Constitution or federal law

### Monell Liability
A municipality may be liable under § 1983 for a constitutional deprivation caused by an official policy or custom (*Monell v. Department of Social Services*, 436 U.S. 658 (1978)).

### Federal Civil Rights Statutes
- **18 U.S.C. § 242** — Criminal deprivation of rights under color of law
- **18 U.S.C. § 241** — Conspiracy against rights
- **42 U.S.C. § 14141** (now 34 U.S.C. § 12601) — Pattern or practice authority (DOJ)
- **Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964** — Prohibits discrimination in federally funded programs
- **Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)** — Requires reasonable accommodation in law enforcement activities

### Racial Profiling
Racially-motivated stops and searches violate the Equal Protection Clause and the Fourth Amendment. The DOJ investigates patterns or practices of discriminatory policing.

## Police Accountability & Oversight

### Internal Affairs
Most agencies maintain internal affairs divisions responsible for investigating allegations of officer misconduct and policy violations.

### Civilian Oversight
Many jurisdictions have established civilian oversight bodies, including:
- Civilian review boards (complaint review)
- Police commissions (policy oversight)
- Inspector general offices (auditing and investigation)

### Consent Decrees & DOJ Pattern-or-Practice Investigations
Under 34 U.S.C. § 12601, the Department of Justice may investigate and seek court-enforceable agreements (consent decrees) with police agencies engaged in patterns or practices of constitutional violations.

### Body-Worn Cameras
Many states and localities have enacted body-worn camera (BWC) laws requiring officers to record police-citizen encounters and establishing retention and access policies.

### Officers' Legal Protections
- **Law Enforcement Officers' Bill of Rights (LEOBR)** — Enacted in some states, providing procedural protections to officers under internal investigation
- **Collective bargaining agreements** — May provide additional procedural rights
- **Indemnification** — Governments typically indemnify officers for judgments arising from on-duty conduct

## Criminal Procedure

### Investigative Stage
1. Complaint/report → Initial investigation
2. Probable cause determination
3. Arrest or citation
4. Booking and initial appearance

### Pre-Trial Stage
1. Bail/detention hearing
2. Grand jury (federal felonies; optional in many states)
3. Preliminary hearing (probable cause determination)
4. Arraignment (formal charges, plea entered)
5. Discovery and disclosure (Brady/Giglio obligations)
6. Pre-trial motions (suppression, dismissal)

### Brady/Giglio Obligations
- ***Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963)** — Prosecution must disclose material exculpatory evidence
- ***Giglio v. United States*, 405 U.S. 150 (1972)** — Prosecution must disclose impeachment evidence, including officer credibility issues ("Giglio lists")

### Trial & Post-Trial
1. Jury selection (voir dire)
2. Opening statements
3. Presentation of evidence
4. Closing arguments
5. Jury instructions
6. Verdict
7. Sentencing
8. Appeals

## Evidence Law

### Admissibility Standards
- **Federal Rules of Evidence (FRE)** govern federal proceedings; states have analogous rules
- Evidence must be relevant (FRE 401), not unfairly prejudicial (FRE 403), and not otherwise excludable

### Exclusionary Rule
Evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment is generally inadmissible (*Mapp v. Ohio*). The rule deters police misconduct.

**Good Faith Exception:** Evidence obtained by officers acting in good-faith reliance on a facially valid warrant is not excluded even if the warrant is later found defective (*United States v. Leon*, 468 U.S. 897 (1984)).

**Inevitable Discovery:** Illegally obtained evidence is admissible if it would inevitably have been discovered through lawful means (*Nix v. Williams*, 467 U.S. 431 (1984)).

### Chain of Custody
Physical evidence must be authenticated by establishing a proper chain of custody — documenting every person who handled the evidence from collection to courtroom.

### Digital Evidence
- **Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), 18 U.S.C. § 1030** — Governs unauthorized computer access
- Digital forensics standards (NIST Guidelines for Evidence Collection)
- Hash values used to authenticate digital files
- Metadata as evidence

## Victims' Rights

### Federal Framework
- **Crime Victims' Rights Act (CVRA), 18 U.S.C. § 3771** — Provides federal crime victims with rights including: to be reasonably protected; to be notified of proceedings; to be present at public court proceedings; to be heard at sentencing; to restitution; and to be treated with fairness and respect
- **Violence Against Women Act (VAWA)** — Federal funding and protections for domestic violence victims
- **Victims of Crime Act (VOCA)** — Federal Crime Victims Fund for state victim assistance programs

### Restitution
Courts may order defendants to pay restitution to victims to compensate for losses. Mandatory in certain federal crimes (18 U.S.C. §§ 3663, 3663A).

### Victim-Witness Assistance Programs
Most U.S. Attorneys' offices and prosecutor offices operate victim-witness assistance programs providing notification, support, and courtroom assistance.

## Juvenile Law Enforcement

### Juvenile Justice System
The juvenile justice system is separate from the adult criminal system and operates under parens patriae principles. Key distinctions:
- Confidentiality of records and proceedings (varies by state)
- Emphasis on rehabilitation over punishment
- Different terminology (delinquency adjudication, not conviction)

### Rights of Juvenile Suspects
- Fourth Amendment applies fully to juveniles
- Miranda applies to custodial interrogation of juveniles (*In re Gault*, 387 U.S. 1 (1967))
- Right to counsel in delinquency proceedings

### Transfer to Adult Court
Juveniles accused of serious offenses may be transferred (waived) to adult court through judicial waiver, prosecutorial direct file, or statutory exclusion, depending on state law.

### Diversion Programs
Law enforcement agencies often have authority to divert juvenile offenders from formal proceedings to community-based diversion programs, counseling, or restorative justice.

## Specialized Law Enforcement Areas

### Domestic Violence
- **Mandatory Arrest Laws** — Many states require arrest when officers have probable cause to believe domestic violence occurred
- Lethality assessments and safety planning
- Emergency Protective Orders (EPOs)
- Federal prohibition on firearms possession for persons subject to domestic violence restraining orders (18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(8)–(9))

### Human Trafficking
- **Trafficking Victims Protection Act (TVPA), 22 U.S.C. § 7101 et seq.** — Federal anti-trafficking framework
- 18 U.S.C. §§ 1581–1597 — Federal criminal prohibitions
- Task force approach: FBI, HSI, local agencies

### Narcotics Enforcement
- **Controlled Substances Act (CSA), 21 U.S.C. § 801 et seq.** — Federal drug scheduling and enforcement
- Asset forfeiture under 21 U.S.C. § 881
- Multi-jurisdictional drug task forces

### Gang Enforcement
- RICO Act (18 U.S.C. §§ 1961–1968) applied to criminal street gangs
- California Street Terrorism Enforcement and Prevention (STEP) Act as model for state gang enhancement statutes
- Gang injunctions (civil nuisance abatement)

### Mental Health Crisis Response
- Crisis Intervention Team (CIT) programs
- Involuntary civil commitment standards (varies by state)
- ADA obligations in mental health encounters

## International Law Enforcement Cooperation

### Treaties & Agreements
- **Mutual Legal Assistance Treaties (MLATs)** — Bilateral agreements for sharing evidence and investigative assistance
- **Extradition Treaties** — Formal process for returning fugitives from foreign countries
- **INTERPOL** — International criminal police organization facilitating cooperation (Red Notices, diffusions)
- **EUROPOL** — European Union law enforcement cooperation agency

### Federal Frameworks
- **International Law Enforcement Academy (ILEA)** — U.S.-funded training for foreign law enforcement
- **Kleptocracy Asset Recovery Initiative** — DOJ recovery of foreign corruption proceeds
- **MLAT requests** — Formal process under 28 U.S.C. § 1782

### Cloud Act (2018)
The Clarifying Lawful Overseas Use of Data Act allows U.S. law enforcement to access data stored abroad by U.S. companies under certain conditions and creates a framework for executive agreements with foreign governments.

## Glossary

**Arrest** — The taking of a person into custody by legal authority, depriving them of their freedom.

**Articulable Suspicion (Reasonable Suspicion)** — Specific, articulable facts that give an officer reasonable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot; less than probable cause.

**Brady Material** — Exculpatory or impeachment evidence that the prosecution must disclose to the defense.

**Chain of Custody** — A chronological documentation showing the seizure, custody, control, transfer, and disposition of evidence.

**Color of Law** — Action taken under the authority of law or apparent authority of law.

**Consent Decree** — A court-enforceable agreement between a government agency and another party resolving allegations of misconduct.

**Curtilage** — The area immediately surrounding a home that receives Fourth Amendment protections similar to the home itself.

**Exculpatory Evidence** — Evidence tending to establish a defendant's innocence.

**Exigent Circumstances** — Emergency conditions that justify a warrantless search or arrest.

**Grand Jury** — A group of citizens that reviews evidence to determine whether probable cause exists to indict a person for a crime.

**Habeas Corpus** — A writ requiring a person detained by the government to be brought before a court to determine the legality of detention.

**Impeachment Evidence** — Evidence used to challenge the credibility of a witness.

**Indictment** — A formal accusation by a grand jury that there is probable cause to believe a person committed a crime.

**Informant** — A person who provides information to law enforcement, often confidentially.

**Miranda Warning** — Advisement of constitutional rights required before custodial interrogation.

**Monell Liability** — Municipal liability under § 1983 based on official policy or custom.

**Plain View Doctrine** — Evidence in plain view of an officer lawfully present may be seized without a warrant.

**Probable Cause** — A reasonable belief, based on articulable facts, that a crime has been or is being committed.

**Qualified Immunity** — A defense available to government officials protecting them from § 1983 liability unless they violated clearly established law.

**Reasonable Suspicion** — Less than probable cause; specific, articulable facts and rational inferences that criminal activity may be occurring.

**Warrant** — A written order issued by a court authorizing law enforcement to conduct a search, make an arrest, or perform some other action.
